Hackathon at Community over Code Glasgow 2026 — Retrospective

We held a hackathon at Community over Code Glasgow 2026 (October 11–14, Hilton Glasgow). Projects brought real tasks and sat down together — new and experienced contributors alike — to work on them across the three days. This page is a retrospective of how it went. View the full poster.

What Happened

The hackathon ran throughout the conference (Monday through Wednesday) in the Wee Dram Room, with projects sharing the space across half-day slots. The room was busy every day, with a steady mix of focused project work and new people wandering in to find out how to get involved. Several project leads noted that the real value was as much in the face-to-face community time as in the code that got written — and that new contributors turned up as a direct result.

Below is what participating projects reported afterwards. Where a project hasn’t reported yet, we’ll add their notes as they come in.

Participating Projects — What They Got Done

Airflow

Three first-time contributors joined the Airflow session, and one small contribution came out of it: apache/airflow#74585. The team’s own takeaway: come better prepared next time, with more small, ready-to-implement issues.

Fluss

Around 10–12 people came by, including contributors from other Apache projects and engineers trying Fluss for the first time. Six PRs were opened during or right after the hackathon:

  • Flink integration: #4630, #4634, #4635
  • Python client and user guides: #4631
  • Elixir client: #4628, #4633

The team said the most valuable part was collaboration across Apache projects. Flink contributors reviewed Fluss’s VARIANT implementation (and caught a correctness issue) and its materialized table integration. Those conversations also led to an upstream Flink proposal that lets queries override or disable watermarks, motivated by the Fluss connector work, so the fix can help other connectors too. There was also discussion of a native Apache Doris–Fluss integration built on fluss-rust, the project’s Rust SDK. Engineers from several companies spent time building with Fluss and gave feedback on the getting-started experience.

Magpie

Three users and three new contributors attended. Several users applied Magpie’s security skills to their own projects. That surfaced gaps for older, SVN-based projects and a handful of “first-time gotchas,” which have all since been fixed. The session also tested using LLMAO with Magpie, and confirmed that optimizing token usage is essential for people without frontier-model subscriptions. Details and links are in the Magpie dev@ wrap-up.

Mahout

The session brought in users and one first-time contributor, covering how-tos and getting people acclimated, and some PRs are on the way.

NuttX

Around 17 active participants (of ~20 planned), 7 of whom were new faces who first heard about NuttX at the conference. The session had a concrete goal: port the 1993 open-source release of Doom to the Sony Spresense IoT board — and they pulled it off during the event. There’s even a video of the result. A great example of a focused, demoable hackathon project that doubled as outreach for the NuttX RTOS.

Responsible AI (LLMAO)

The LLMAO session drew users and newcomers for how-tos and getting started, and came away with a to-do list shaped by user feedback.

Sourcelume

A small, productive group of 5 — including 2 new contributors whose first PRs were accepted after the conference. They built a demo exercising all of the project’s functionality as of the conference, and started on end-user tooling to make Sourcelume easier to adopt. Much of the surrounding work carried on over the dev lists afterwards, and the project has since made significant progress on two of its key modules. See the Sourcelume demo, referenced from the Registry page.

Tomcat

A compact group — 3 in person plus 3 joining remotely via a Slack huddle — who used the time to work through several proposals for improvements. Being able to discuss these synchronously was valuable, and the team noted that the hackathon time also set up their Thursday security day nicely: the non-security topics got aired in the hackathon, so the security day could stay focused on security. A good reminder that “time together as a community” is a legitimate and useful hackathon outcome in its own right. Follow-on discussion continued on the dev list.

Tooling (Apache Trusted Releases)

The ATR session brought in users and newcomers for how-tos and onboarding, and left with a to-do list driven by their feedback.

Other participating projects

Nineteen projects booked hackathon slots in all. We haven’t yet heard from Apache Search, Arrow, Doris, Fineract, Gluten, Groovy & Grails, Impala, Spark, Superset, or Wayang. We’ll add their write-ups as they come in.

Lessons Learned

Notes for whoever runs the hackathon in future years. These are deliberately general — they’re about the format and the room, not about any one project or person.

  • Watch the room acoustics. With several projects sharing one room, noise from neighboring tables can be a distraction for groups trying to have a focused technical discussion. Consider more space between tables, a larger room, or a quieter corner for discussion-heavy groups.
  • Keep the space usable. Unattended laptops and belongings left on tables took up room that active participants needed, and people were reluctant to move someone else’s gear. A simple norm — hand abandoned items to the hotel as lost property, and label or clear tables between slots — would help.
  • Check the physical basics early. At least one group lost the use of power outlets in their part of the room. Confirm power, seating, and connectivity in every section of the room before the event starts, and have a venue contact on call to fix problems quickly.
  • “Just being together” is a real outcome. More than one project got as much value from synchronous, face-to-face discussion as from merged code. Set expectations accordingly — a successful hackathon table doesn’t have to produce a pile of PRs to be worthwhile.
  • A concrete, demoable goal works well. The sessions that set a single clear target going in had an easy time showing what they accomplished and used it as outreach afterwards. Encourage projects to come with a well-defined, achievable goal (and ideally something they can demo).
  • New contributors do show up. Several projects connected with first-time contributors who found them at the conference. Have good “first task” and onboarding material ready so that interest converts into contribution.
  • Come prepared with small, ready-to-go issues. Groups that arrived with a short list of small, well-scoped tasks found it easier to get newcomers contributing quickly. Several teams said they’d prepare more next time.
  • The event motivates maintainers too. Having a date on the calendar got maintainers to finish work before the event, and user feedback during the session produced a concrete to-do list afterward.
  • Cross-project collaboration is a big win. Some of the most valuable results came from contributors of other Apache projects reviewing work and spotting improvements upstream. Seating related projects near each other, or scheduling them in the same slot, could encourage more of this.
  • Make it easy to track outcomes. Many valuable discussions don’t turn into PRs right away, especially across projects. A simple shared way to record outcomes and follow-ups would help capture them.
  • The work continues after the event. A lot of the real progress happened on the dev lists in the days and weeks afterward. Build follow-up into the plan rather than treating the hackathon as a one-off.
